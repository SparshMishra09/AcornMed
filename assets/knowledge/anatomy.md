# Anatomy

## Upper Limb — Brachial Plexus
The brachial plexus is formed by the anterior rami of C5–T1. Organization: Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches (mnemonic: "Randy Travis Drinks Cold Beer").
- Roots: C5, C6, C7, C8, T1 emerge between anterior and middle scalene muscles.
- Trunks: C5+C6 = upper trunk; C7 = middle trunk; C8+T1 = lower trunk.
- Divisions: each trunk splits into anterior and posterior divisions behind the clavicle.
- Cords: lateral (anterior divisions of upper + middle), posterior (all posterior divisions), medial (anterior division of lower trunk) — named by relation to axillary artery.
- Terminal branches: musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median, ulnar (mnemonic: "MARMU").

Key branches:
- Dorsal scapular nerve (C5): rhomboids.
- Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7): serratus anterior — injury causes winged scapula.
- Suprascapular nerve (C5–C6): supraspinatus, infraspinatus.
- Thoracodorsal nerve (C6–C8): latissimus dorsi.
- Axillary nerve (C5–C6): deltoid + teres minor; wraps surgical neck of humerus — injured in anterior shoulder dislocation.
- Radial nerve (C5–T1): all extensors of forearm; runs in spiral groove — injured in midshaft humerus fracture → wrist drop.
- Median nerve (C5–T1): most forearm flexors; injured in supracondylar fracture (anterior interosseous) or carpal tunnel; "hand of benediction" when trying to make fist (high lesion), "ape hand" at rest.
- Ulnar nerve (C8–T1): FCU + medial FDP, most intrinsic hand muscles; injured at medial epicondyle ("funny bone") → claw hand (worse with low lesion — ulnar paradox).
- Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C7): biceps, brachialis, coracobrachialis; continues as lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm.

## Upper Limb — Shoulder and Arm
Rotator cuff muscles (mnemonic SITS): Supraspinatus (abduction 0–15°), Infraspinatus (external rotation), Teres minor (external rotation), Subscapularus (internal rotation). Supraspinatus is most commonly torn; empty-can test positive.
Biceps brachii: flexion + supination; musculocutaneous nerve. Triceps: extension; radial nerve.
Cubital fossa contents lateral to medial: biceps Tendon, brachial Artery, median Nerve (mnemonic TAN).

## Upper Limb — Forearm and Hand
Carpal bones proximal row (lateral→medial): Scaphoid, Lunate, Triquetrum, Pisiform ("Some Lovers Try Positions"); distal row: Trapezium, Trapezoid, Capitate, Hamate ("Private Positions"). Scaphoid fracture: most common carpal fracture, tenderness in anatomical snuffbox, risk of avascular necrosis of proximal pole. Lunate dislocation: most common carpal dislocation, may compress median nerve.
Hand muscles: thenar (abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis — median nerve, except adductor pollicis = ulnar), hypothenar (ulnar nerve), lumbricals 1–2 median, 3–4 ulnar. All interossei ulnar nerve (PAD = palmar adduct, DAB = dorsal abduct).
Palmar arches: superficial (ulnar artery dominant), deep (radial artery dominant).

## Lower Limb — Hip and Thigh
Femoral triangle boundaries: inguinal ligament (superior), sartorius (lateral), adductor longus (medial). Contents lateral→medial: femoral Nerve, femoral Artery, femoral Vein, empty space, lymphatics (mnemonic NAVEL). Femoral nerve (L2–L4) is outside the sheath.
Femoral artery becomes popliteal artery at adductor hiatus. Profunda femoris gives medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries (supply femoral head — damaged in femoral neck fractures → avascular necrosis).
Quadriceps femoris (femoral nerve): rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius — knee extension; rectus femoris also flexes hip. Patellar reflex L2–L4 (mainly L4).
Hamstrings (tibial division of sciatic nerve): biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus — hip extension, knee flexion. Short head of biceps femoris supplied by common fibular division.
Adductor compartment (obturator nerve L2–L4): adductor longus, brevis, magnus (hamstring part = tibial nerve), gracilis.

## Lower Limb — Leg and Foot
Sciatic nerve (L4–S3): largest nerve; usually splits at apex of popliteal fossa into tibial and common fibular nerves.
Common fibular nerve: wraps around neck of fibula — most commonly injured nerve in lower limb (fibular neck fracture, knee dislocation) → foot drop (loss of dorsiflexion + eversion), sensory loss over dorsum of foot.
Tibial nerve: posterior compartment (gastrocnemius, soleus, tibialis posterior, FDL, FHL) — plantar flexion, inversion; injury → inability to stand on tiptoe.
Anterior compartment: deep fibular nerve — tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL (dorsiflexion). Lateral compartment: superficial fibular nerve — fibularis longus/brevis (eversion).
Knee: ACL prevents anterior tibial translation (anterior drawer, Lachman test); PCL prevents posterior translation (posterior drawer, dashboard injury). Menisci: medial C-shaped, lateral O-shaped ("Medial C, Lateral O"). Unhappy triad (O'Donoghue): ACL + MCL + medial meniscus.
Ankle: inversion sprain most common (ATFL first). Dorsalis pedis pulse lateral to EHL tendon. Posterior tibial pulse behind medial malleolus. Tarsal tunnel: tibial nerve behind medial malleolus (mnemonic Tom, Dick, And Very Nervous Harry — Tibialis posterior, flexor Digitorum longus, Artery, Vein, Nerve, flexor Hallucis longus).

## Thorax — Heart
Heart chambers: right atrium receives SVC, IVC, coronary sinus. Right ventricle: tricuspid valve, moderator band, gives rise to pulmonary trunk. Left atrium: most posterior chamber, receives 4 pulmonary veins; enlargement compresses esophagus (dysphagia) and left recurrent laryngeal nerve (Ortner syndrome — hoarseness). Left ventricle: thickest wall, mitral (bicuspid) valve.
Coronary arteries: right coronary artery (RCA) → SA node (60%), AV node (80–90%); posterior descending artery usually from RCA (right-dominant 85%). LAD (left anterior descending) supplies anterior wall of LV and anterior 2/3 of interventricular septum — "widow maker" when occluded. Circumflex → lateral wall of LV.
Valve auscultation: Aortic = right 2nd intercostal space; Pulmonic = left 2nd; Tricuspid = left lower sternal border; Mitral = apex, 5th intercostal space midclavicular line ("All Physicians Take Money").
Pericardium: fibrous + serous (parietal/visceral). Transverse pericardial sinus behind ascending aorta/pulmonary trunk — used surgically to clamp great vessels. Phrenic nerves (C3–C5) run between fibrous pericardium and mediastinal pleura; pericarditis pain referred to shoulder (C3–C5 dermatome).
Coronary sinus drains into right atrium. Great cardiac vein runs with LAD.

## Thorax — Lungs and Pleura
Right lung: 3 lobes (upper, middle, lower), 2 fissures (horizontal + oblique), shorter/wider/more vertical main bronchus → foreign bodies more often lodge here. Left lung: 2 lobes, lingula, cardiac notch.
Bronchopulmonary segments: right 10, left 8–10; each supplied by segmental bronchus + tertiary pulmonary artery — surgically resectable units.
Aspiration: upright → posterior basal segment of right lower lobe; supine → posterior segment of right upper lobe or superior segment of right lower lobe.
Pleura: visceral (lung surface) and parietal (sensitive to pain via intercostal/phrenic nerves). Costodiaphragmatic recess: lowest point, fluid collects here. Lung apex extends 2–3 cm above medial clavicle — Pancoast tumor may compress sympathetic chain (Horner syndrome) or lower trunk of brachial plexus.
Hilum arrangement right: eparterial bronchus above pulmonary artery. Pulmonary veins are most anterior and inferior at hilum.

## Thorax — Mediastinum
Superior mediastinum (above sternal angle T4/T5): thymus, great vessels, trachea, esophagus, thoracic duct, vagus, phrenic nerves.
Recurrent laryngeal nerves: left loops under aortic arch (ligamentum arteriosum), right loops under subclavian artery — left more vulnerable in thoracic surgery/mediastinal tumors.
Posterior mediastinum: esophagus, descending aorta, thoracic duct, azygos system, vagus nerves. Esophagus constrictions: cricopharyngeus (15 cm), aortic arch (22.5 cm), left main bronchus (27.5 cm), diaphragmatic hiatus T10 (40 cm).
Diaphragm openings: T8 IVC, T10 esophagus + vagus, T12 aorta + thoracic duct + azygos ("I 8 10 EGGs At 12").

## Abdomen — Peritoneum and GI Tract
Foregut (celiac trunk T12): esophagus to D2, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen. Midgut (SMA L1): D2 to proximal 2/3 transverse colon. Hindgut (IMA L3): distal 1/3 transverse colon to upper rectum.
Stomach: cardia, fundus, body, pylorus (antrum + canal). Lesser curvature supplied by left/right gastric arteries; greater by gastroepiploic + short gastric. Celiac trunk branches: left gastric, splenic, common hepatic.
Duodenum: D1 (intraperitoneal, ulcer-prone), D2 (ampulla of Vater — major duodenal papilla), D3 (crossed by SMA — SMA syndrome), D4. Suspensory muscle of duodenum (ligament of Treitz) marks duodenojejunal flexure.
Appendix: base at confluence of taeniae coli; McBurney point (1/3 from ASIS to umbilicus). Most common position: retrocecal.
Liver: 8 Couinaud segments; functional division by Cantlie line (IVC to gallbladder fossa). Portal triad in hepatoduodenal ligament: portal vein (posterior), hepatic artery proper (medial), common bile duct (lateral) — Pringle maneuver compresses here. Bare area of liver contacts diaphragm.
Portosystemic anastomoses (portal hypertension): esophageal varices (left gastric ↔ azygos), caput medusae (paraumbilical ↔ superficial epigastric), anorectal varices (superior rectal ↔ middle/inferior rectal), retroperitoneal (veins of Retzius).

## Abdomen — Kidneys and Retroperitoneum
Kidneys: T12–L3, right slightly lower (liver). Hilum order anterior→posterior: Vein, Artery, Pelvis (VAP). Renal artery posterior to renal vein. Left renal vein longer, crosses anterior to aorta, receives left gonadal vein — nutcracker syndrome when compressed between aorta and SMA.
Ureters: cross pelvic brim at bifurcation of common iliac; constrictions at pelviureteric junction, pelvic brim, vesicoureteric junction — kidney stones lodge here. "Water under the bridge": ureter passes under uterine artery (female) / vas deferens (male).
Adrenal glands: right triangular, posterior to IVC; left crescentic, medial to upper kidney. Arterial supply: superior (inferior phrenic), middle (aorta), inferior (renal artery). Venous drainage: right adrenal → IVC directly; left adrenal → left renal vein.
Retroperitoneal structures (mnemonic SAD PUCKER): Suprarenal glands, Aorta/IVC, Duodenum (D2–D4), Pancreas, Ureters, Colon (ascending/descending), Kidneys, Esophagus, Rectum.

## Pelvis and Perineum
Uterus: anteverted/anteflexed normally; supported by cardinal (transverse cervical) ligaments (main support), uterosacral, round ligament (passes through inguinal canal), broad ligament. Ureter passes under uterine artery ~2 cm lateral to cervix — at risk in hysterectomy.
Ovary: suspended by mesovarium; blood supply from ovarian artery (aorta) + ovarian branch of uterine artery. Ovarian fossa bounded by external iliac vessels, ureter posteriorly.
Prostate: surrounds prostatic urethra; zones — peripheral (most cancers), central, transition (BPH). Ejaculatory ducts open into prostatic utricle region.
Perineum: urogenital triangle anterior, anal triangle posterior. Ischioanal fossa lateral to anal canal. Pudendal nerve (S2–S4): exits greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, hooks around ischial spine, enters lesser sciatic foramen (Alcock canal) — blocked at ischial spine for obstetric anesthesia.
Pelvic floor: levator ani (puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus) + coccygeus. Puborectalis maintains anorectal angle (continence).

## Head and Neck — Cranial Nerves
Cranial nerves (mnemonic "Oh Oh Oh To Touch And Feel Very Good Velvet, Such Heaven"): I Olfactory, II Optic, III Oculomotor, IV Trochlear, V Trigeminal, VI Abducens, VII Facial, VIII Vestibulocochlear, IX Glossopharyngeal, X Vagus, XI Accessory, XII Hypoglossal.
Sensory/motor (mnemonic "Some Say Marry Money But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter Most"): I S, II S, III M, IV M, V both, VI M, VII both, VIII S, IX both, X both, XI M, XII M.
- CN III: superior rectus, inferior rectus, medial rectus, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae; parasympathetic to sphincter pupillae + ciliary muscle. Palsy: "down and out" eye, ptosis, mydriasis (parasympathetic fibers superficial → compressed by posterior communicating artery aneurysm).
- CN IV: superior oblique (intorsion, depression when adducted); longest intracranial course, only CN exiting dorsally; palsy → trouble with stairs/reading, head tilt away.
- CN V: V1 ophthalmic (sensory, superior orbital fissure), V2 maxillary (sensory, foramen rotundum), V3 mandibular (sensory + motor to muscles of mastication, foramen ovale). Trigeminal neuralgia most often V2/V3.
- CN VI: lateral rectus; longest intracranial course over petrous apex → raised ICP causes lateral rectus palsy (false localizing sign).
- CN VII: muscles of facial expression, stapedius, posterior belly digastric, stylohyoid; taste anterior 2/3 tongue (chorda tympani); parasympathetic to lacrimal, submandibular, sublingual glands. Lesion at stylomastoid foramen → Bell palsy (whole ipsilateral face). UMN lesion spares forehead (bilateral cortical input to upper face).
- CN IX: stylopharyngeus, parotid gland (via otic ganglion), taste/sensation posterior 1/3 tongue, carotid body/sinus. Gag reflex afferent.
- CN X: pharynx/larynx muscles (except stylopharyngeus = IX, tensor veli palatini = V3); gag reflex efferent; parasympathetic to thoracoabdominal viscera. Uvula deviates away from side of lesion.
- CN XI: SCM + trapezius; spinal root through foramen magnum then jugular foramen.
- CN XII: tongue muscles except palatoglossus (X); tongue deviates toward side of lesion.

## Head and Neck — Triangles, Vasculature, Glands
Anterior triangle: submental, submandibular, carotid, muscular. Posterior triangle: occipital, supraclavicular (subclavian). CN XI crosses posterior triangle — vulnerable in lymph node biopsy → trapezius weakness.
Carotid triangle: common carotid bifurcation at C4 (upper border of thyroid cartilage). Carotid sinus (baroreceptor, CN IX) at bifurcation; carotid body (chemoreceptor) in bifurcation.
External carotid branches (mnemonic "Some Anatomists Like Freaking Out Poor Medical Students"): superior thyroid, ascending pharyngeal, lingual, facial, occipital, posterior auricular, maxillary, superficial temporal.
Thyroid gland: C5–T1; isthmus over tracheal rings 2–4. Arterial supply: superior thyroid (external carotid), inferior thyroid (thyrocervical trunk). Recurrent laryngeal nerves run in tracheoesophageal groove — at risk in thyroidectomy; external branch of superior laryngeal nerve runs with superior thyroid artery (cricothyroid muscle — voice pitch).
Parathyroids: usually 4, on posterior thyroid. Thyroid ima artery (variant) from brachiocephalic/aorta.
Parotid gland: CN VII passes through it (divides into 5 terminal branches: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical — "To Zanzibar By Motor Car"); retromandibular vein + external carotid also within. Parotid duct crosses masseter, pierces buccinator, opens opposite upper 2nd molar.
Submandibular duct (Wharton): opens at sublingual papilla beside frenulum; crossed by lingual nerve (hooks around it).
Tongue: anterior 2/3 general sensation V3 (lingual nerve), taste VII (chorda tympani); posterior 1/3 both IX; vallate papillae taste IX. Motor XII (except palatoglossus X).

## Neuroanatomy — Brain and Ventricles
Cerebral lobes: frontal (motor, personality, Broca area — inferior frontal gyrus, dominant hemisphere), parietal (sensory, dominant = Gerstmann syndrome if angular gyrus lesion; nondominant = hemispatial neglect), temporal (hearing, Wernicke area — posterior superior temporal gyrus, dominant; memory via hippocampus), occipital (vision).
Internal capsule: anterior limb, genu (corticobulbar), posterior limb (corticospinal — arm anterior, leg posterior; thalamocortical fibers), retrolenticular (optic radiations), sublenticular (auditory radiations). Lenticulostriate arteries (from MCA) supply it — "arteries of cerebral hemorrhage" in hypertension.
Circle of Willis: anterior communicating connects ACAs; posterior communicating connects ICA to PCA. Most common aneurysm site: anterior communicating artery. Berry aneurysms associated with ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos.
Ventricles: lateral (1, 2) → foramen of Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius, in midbrain) → fourth ventricle → foramina of Luschka (lateral) + Magendie (median) → subarachnoid space. CSF produced by choroid plexus (~500 mL/day); absorbed by arachnoid granulations into superior sagittal sinus. Obstruction at aqueduct → triventricular hydrocephalus.
Brainstem: midbrain (CN III, IV; cerebral peduncles, superior/inferior colliculi), pons (CN V–VIII; middle cerebellar peduncle), medulla (CN IX–XII; pyramids, olives).
Cavernous sinus contents: CN III, IV, V1, V2 in lateral wall; CN VI and internal carotid artery (with sympathetic plexus) run through center. CN VI most vulnerable in cavernous sinus thrombosis. Danger triangle of face → cavernous sinus via facial vein/ophthalmic veins.

## Neuroanatomy — Spinal Cord and Tracts
Spinal cord tracts:
- Dorsal columns (fasciculus gracilis medial = legs; cuneatus lateral = arms): fine touch, vibration, proprioception; cross in medulla (internal arcuate fibers → medial lemniscus).
- Spinothalamic tract (anterolateral): pain, temperature, crude touch; cross within 1–2 segments via anterior white commissure.
- Corticospinal tract: motor; 85% cross at pyramidal decussation (lateral CST), 15% anterior CST.
- Spinocerebellar tracts: unconscious proprioception.
Blood supply: anterior spinal artery (anterior 2/3 — motor + spinothalamic), posterior spinal arteries (posterior 1/3 — dorsal columns). Artery of Adamkiewicz (T8–L1, usually left) supplies lower cord. Anterior spinal artery syndrome: bilateral motor + pain/temp loss, proprioception spared.
Spinal cord syndromes:
- Brown-Séquard (hemisection): ipsilateral UMN weakness + proprioception loss; contralateral pain/temp loss (1–2 levels below).
- Syringomyelia: central cavitation, crosses anterior white commissure → bilateral loss of pain/temp in cape distribution (upper limbs), touch preserved ("dissociated sensory loss").
- Subacute combined degeneration (B12 deficiency): dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal tracts.
- Tabes dorsalis (tertiary syphilis): dorsal columns + roots.
Dermatomes: C4 shoulder, C6 thumb, C7 middle finger, C8 little finger, T4 nipples, T10 umbilicus, L1 inguinal, L4 medial leg/knee, L5 dorsum of foot/big toe, S1 lateral foot/sole, S2–S4 perineum.
Reflexes: biceps C5–C6, brachioradialis C6, triceps C7–C8, patellar L3–L4, Achilles S1–S2.

## Embryology High-Yield
Pharyngeal arches: 1st (CN V — muscles of mastication, Meckel cartilage → mandible/malleus/incus), 2nd (CN VII — muscles of facial expression, stapes, styloid, lesser horn hyoid), 3rd (CN IX — stylopharyngeus, greater horn hyoid), 4th (CN X superior laryngeal — pharyngeal constrictors, thyroid cartilage), 6th (CN X recurrent laryngeal — intrinsic laryngeal muscles).
Aortic arch derivatives: 1st = maxillary artery, 2nd = stapedial/hyoid, 3rd = common carotid + proximal internal carotid, 4th left = aortic arch, 4th right = proximal right subclavian, 6th = pulmonary arteries + ductus arteriosus (left).
GI embryology: vitelline duct remnant = Meckel diverticulum (rule of 2s: 2% population, 2 feet from ileocecal valve, 2 inches long, 2 types of ectopic tissue — gastric/pancreatic, presents before age 2). Midgut herniates at week 6, returns week 10 rotating 270° counterclockwise around SMA.
Congenital heart: most common cyanotic CHD = tetralogy of Fallot (PROVe: Pulmonary stenosis, RVH, Overriding aorta, VSD). Most common CHD overall = VSD. Coarctation associated with Turner syndrome + bicuspid aortic valve. Transposition of great vessels: most common cause of cyanotic CHD in neonates; needs PDA/VSD for survival; "egg on string" X-ray.
Diaphragm hernias: Bochdalek (posterolateral, left, most common congenital), Morgagni (anterior/parasternal).
